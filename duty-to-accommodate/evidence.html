Compare the right things.

The new sources distinguish (1) employee outcomes, (2) case-processing measurements, (3) completed process changes, and (4) future promises. Strong results can indicate a promising organization, but do not establish which specific practice produced them.

Large sample and high satisfaction. The adaptive-technology support program provides a concrete practice to study; it has not been shown to cause the survey result.

Combined survey and audit evidence

Public Service Commission

Q86 78% (n=150) · Q87 83% (n=120) · Q88 81% (n=525).

The 2026 audit found a 31-day average case closure. Its newer yes-by-default and standardized tracking commitments remain prospective.

High results, smaller samples

Canadian Human Rights Commission

Q86 92% (n=60) · Q87 94% (n=55) · Q88 90% (n=200).

Very high results, but Q86 and Q87 have small sample sizes; the organization's own plan still describes process uncertainty. Not yet a proven practice model.

Requesting comfort

Communications Security Establishment

Q86 78% (n=260) · Q87 80% (n=205) · Q88 91% (n=1,740).

High comfort requesting accommodation among a substantial number of respondents. The practice behind this outcome has not been identified in the reviewed documents.

Speed is not the same as satisfaction

Canada School of Public Service

Q87 66% (n=60) · 24 days average audited case closure.

The fastest audited closure average coexists with relatively low satisfaction in the 2024 PSES. Both results merit examination; they measure different things.

Analytical framework

What to investigate next

Strength signals

Centralized expertise; financing separated from individual supervisors' budgets; standardized and accessible guidance; portable accommodation agreements; interim supports; service standards; independent feedback; reported completion and effectiveness.

Weakness signals

Confusing intake points; repeated medical documentation without justification; manager-only untracked cases; inconsistent approvals; delayed temporary measures; fear of reprisal or career harm; unresolved needs; low trust; plans with no service or results indicators.

Legal distinction.

The individual duty to accommodate under human rights law is distinct from proactive barrier-removal and reporting obligations under the Accessible Canada Act, and from preventive health and safety obligations. Survey patterns and delayed cases can justify investigation, but are not themselves findings of statutory breach. Tribunal decisions should be matched to employee accommodation cases; cases involving inmates or service recipients must not be presented as workplace precedents.

What is missing from public reporting?

Comparable organization-level data remain scarce on median days until effective accommodations, interim measures, number of denied or withdrawn requests, reasons for delay, repeated documentation, unresolved needs, outcomes after transfers and whether managers' informal requests are centrally recorded. This catalogue also identifies planned audits and evaluations, which are leads—not results.
